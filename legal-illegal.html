Traffic Light Model

legal or illegal

Three academic AI-use scenarios - one red, one yellow, one green. Can you tell which is which?

01 - Case Files

Guess the Category

Case File A

The Ghostwritten Proposal

A Master's student must submit a 5000-word research proposal as a graded individual assignment. They ask an AI chatbot to generate the full text, complete with citations to academic papers they have never read. The student submits the AI output unchanged and does not mention any AI assistance. During review, the professor discovers that several cited sources do not exist.

Case File B

The Slide Factory

A four-person group is preparing a graded presentation on a business case study. Each member is responsible for one section. One member uses AI to generate their entire set of slides, including analysis and recommendations, without discussing the content with the group. The presentation earns a good mark, but that student struggles to answer follow-up questions about their own section during the Q&A.

Case File C

The Tool-Assisted Researcher

A student writing an individual term paper uses AI to explain unfamiliar theories, generate discussion questions to test their understanding, and get feedback on paragraph clarity. Every argument in the final paper is written and fact-checked by the student against primary sources. The student includes an "AI use disclosure" in the appendix describing which tools were used and how.

02 - Discussion

Where Do You Draw the Line?

Each of the three scenarios above represents a different traffic light category: green (acceptable use), yellow (questionable use), or red (forbidden use). Discuss which case belongs where and why. The traffic light model reminds us that AI use in academia is rarely a purely technical question - it is also about trust, authorship, fairness, and transparency. The same tool can be acceptable, doubtful, or forbidden depending on how much it contributes to the assessed work and whether its role is disclosed.
